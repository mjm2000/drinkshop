Is food required?

Three different questions get conflated: (1) Do city health codes require a food permit? (2) Must you sell food to customers? (3) Does TABC or zoning force a kitchen/entrees? The answers differ by use type.

Question Energy drink shop Coffee shop Alcohol bar
City food-establishment permit needed? Yes — custom prep (ice, bases, dairy alt, blending) triggers food service rules[2] Yes — espresso, steamed milk, and ice prep require a permit[2] Yes — bar prep + any food service requires a permit[11]
Must you sell food to operate? No — drinks-only model is valid No — beverage-only coffee shops are common Yes — for most on-premise liquor licenses
Kitchen / hot food required? No — no commercial cooking equipment needed No — unless you choose a full food program Yes — commercial cooking + multiple entrees for TABC FB[14][15]

Grease trap / hood needed? Unlikely — beverage-only; confirm with plan reviewer[2] Sometimes — if no cooking, often avoided; bakery/kitchen adds requirement Yes — full kitchen for entree program

Energy shop — VOLT BAR

Food is not required for the business model or for staying classified as a restaurant (non-bar) use under Dallas/Frisco zoning.[1][4]

You still need a food establishment permit because staff prepare custom drinks — that is beverage food service, not a retail-only exemption.

Bottled & imported cans are sealed retail; they do not trigger extra kitchen rules beyond normal storage and labeling.

Optional: Prepackaged snacks (Japanese kit-kat, protein bars, chips) increase basket size with zero kitchen build-out. Avoid hot food unless you deliberately add a kitchen line.

Coffee shop

Food is not required to open or maintain a coffee-focused permit. Many DFW shops run beverage-only with prepackaged pastries delivered from a commissary.

Health departments regulate milk, ice, and espresso prep — same permit family as VOLT BAR, but without TABC overlay.[2]

Optional upgrade path: Adding fresh food or a kitchen increases COGS, grease interceptor cost, and inspection scope — usually done to grow afternoon ticket, not because the law demands it.

If adding alcohol: Wine/beer or mixed drinks trigger TABC food rules — food becomes mandatory at that point.[14]

Alcohol bar

Food is required for a typical Texas on-premise liquor operation. TABC Food and Beverage Certificate rules require permanent food service facilities, commercial cooking equipment, and multiple entrees available whenever alcohol is served.[14][15]

Food must be available whenever alcohol is served. Chips and nuts from a vending machine do not qualify as food service.[15]

Alternative path: Keep alcohol ≤60% of gross receipts to qualify for FB without full restaurant kitchen — still requires documented food sales, not alcohol-only.[14]

Zoning layer: Dallas defines bar/lounge as ≥75% alcohol revenue — operating alcohol-primary without adequate food pushes you into bar SUP territory, not restaurant-by-right.[1]

VOLT BAR takeaway

Staying non-alcoholic and beverage-focused avoids TABC food mandates entirely. You need a health permit for drink prep, but you do not need a kitchen, hood, grease trap, or entree menu — a major cost and setup advantage over opening as a bar. Optional prepackaged snacks pair naturally with bottled/import energy sales.

Profitability — pros & cons

Energy drink shop

Pros
  • High gross margin — 65–75% on custom bases, syrups, and add-ins; no expensive alcohol COGS
  • Higher avg ticket — $8–14 vs. $5–8 coffee; upsells (extra caffeine, functional add-ins) are pure margin
  • Extended dayparts — afternoon + late-night revenue without competing for 6 AM rush
  • Underserved niche — less direct competition than coffee; category still forming in DFW
  • Repeat frequency potential — gym/gamer demo may visit 3–5×/week if loyalty sticks
Cons
  • No proven comps — revenue projections are modeled, not franchise-validated
  • Category risk — energy drink fatigue or novelty wear-off could slow returns
  • Lower ceiling than bar — no alcohol markup ($15–30+ cocktail tickets)
  • Late-night labor — premium pay for 9 PM–1 AM shifts eats into net margin
  • Caffeine liability perception — may limit some corporate or school partnerships

Coffee shop

Pros
  • Proven habit — daily morning traffic; customers already budget for coffee
  • Office corridor fit — Richardson, Legacy, telecom parks deliver predictable lunch volume
  • Stable 60–70% margins — well-understood COGS on espresso and milk
  • Franchise playbooks exist — Scooter's, Dutch Bros, 7 Brew models to study
  • Add-on pastry/food — increases ticket without full kitchen (prepackaged route)
Cons
  • Low ticket ceiling — $5–8 avg limits revenue per visit
  • DFW oversaturated — drive-through chains on every corner; hard to win share
  • Morning-dependent — dead hours 2–5 PM unless pivoting; weak late-night
  • Commodity perception — difficult to charge premium without strong brand
  • Longer break-even — 18–24 months common in competitive suburban pads

Alcohol bar

Pros
  • Highest revenue per drink — $15–30+ tickets; 70–85% margin on spirits
  • Tips boost staff income — easier to recruit and retain bartenders
  • Nightlife corridors — Deep Ellum / West 7th built for bar economics
  • Event & weekend spikes — game days, concerts drive volume surges
  • Existing bar shells — can inherit build-out and some permit history
Cons
  • 75% alcohol rule — food revenue capped in Dallas/Frisco or use reclassifies[1][9]
  • Dram shop liability — lawsuits from over-service; higher insurance premiums
  • Volatile revenue — weather, trends, and competition swing weekly sales
  • Age gate limits TAM — excludes under-21 (large share of gaming/fitness demo)
  • Security & staffing costs — bouncers, late-night labor, breakage add overhead

Energy — cost summary

Pros: No TABC or dram shop insurance; equipment cheaper than espresso + bar combo; beverage-only keeps grease trap/kitchen costs down.

Cons: Custom lounge build-out adds $15K–$40K vs. basic counter; ice program is ongoing utility cost; novel concept may mean less TI from landlord.

Coffee — cost summary

Pros: Lowest barrier if beverage-only; used equipment market is deep; landlords offer coffee-ready shells in office retail.

Cons: Commercial espresso setup is $8K–$25K alone; franchise royalties 5–8% if not independent; pastry/kitchen adds cost fast.

Bar — cost summary

Pros: Can lease former bar space with existing wells and TABC-ready layout; alcohol turns inventory quickly on busy nights.

Cons: Highest total startup ($350K–$550K+); TABC application timeline and legal fees; security systems and higher insurance are mandatory.

Setup difficulty — pros & cons

Energy drink shop

Easier
  • Zoned as restaurant without drive-in — same path as coffee[1]
  • No TABC application, liquor overlay, or age-verification systems[3]
  • Restaurant by right in Deep Ellum PD 269 — no bar SUP[7]
  • Standard food establishment health permit process[2]
  • Serves all ages — no 21+ door policy or bouncer requirements
Harder
  • Novel use — planners and landlords may not understand "energy drink bar"[4]
  • Late-night (midnight+) may require SUP in PD 842 or proposed Deep Ellum rules[5][6]
  • Must document caffeine policy for liability and staff training (no standard template)
  • Custom mg-display / kiosk tech adds setup complexity
  • Fewer existing "energy bar" shells to inherit — mostly ground-up or coffee conversion

Coffee shop

Easier
  • Best-understood use — fastest CO and health permit approval[2]
  • Landlords actively seek coffee tenants in office and suburban pads
  • Abundant contractor and equipment vendor ecosystem in DFW
  • Minimal zoning friction — permitted broadly[9][10]
  • Many former coffee / smoothie shells available for quick turnaround
Harder
  • Espresso machine install requires plumbing, drainage, electrical load
  • Differentiating in saturated market is a business challenge, not just setup
  • Drive-through pads need stacking lanes — extra site plan review in suburbs
  • Franchise approval process adds months if going that route

Alcohol bar

Easier
  • Nightlife districts expect bar tenants — cultural fit in Deep Ellum[7]
  • Former bar spaces include wells, coolers, and sometimes active SUP[1]
  • Fort Worth MU-2: bars ≤2,000 sq ft permitted by right[8]
  • Established playbook for TABC, security, and late-night ops
Harder
  • SUP/CUP required in many Dallas PDs — public hearing, 3–6+ months[1]
  • TABC licensing timeline + background checks + distance from schools/churches[3]
  • Change-of-use from restaurant to bar triggers full rezoning review[12]
  • SUP conditions: noise limits, no outdoor speakers, security staffing[6]
  • Highest inspection scrutiny — occupancy load, fire, dram shop compliance

Zoning & use classification

Energy drink shop — VOLT BAR

Classified like a restaurant without drive-in service or specialty beverage retail with on-premise prep. Zoned as food service, not as a bar, as long as alcohol is not sold and entertainment is incidental.[1][4]

  • Permitted districts: Commercial, central area, mixed-use (MU-2), most highway retail — same footprint as coffee[1][9]
  • Advantage: Avoids bar SUP/CUP in Dallas PD 269 (Deep Ellum) where bar requires SUP but restaurant is by right[7]
  • Risk: If late-night lounge + DJs dominate, city may scrutinize use as entertainment/bar-adjacent[4]

Coffee shop

Same base zoning category — restaurant/café without drive-through. Most DFW cities treat espresso + milk prep as standard food service.[1][2]

  • Permitted districts: Broadest allowance — office parks, suburban pads, urban walkable strips[9][10]
  • Advantage: Easiest CO path — landlords and inspectors understand the use; morning daypart fits office corridors
  • Limitation: Saturated market in DFW; harder to differentiate; weak late-night unless hybrid model

Alcohol bar

Separate use category. Dallas defines bar/lounge/tavern as ≥75% quarterly gross revenue from on-premise alcohol.[1] Frisco uses the same 75% threshold for reclassification.[9]

  • Permitted districts: Often SUP-only in Dallas PDs; Fort Worth MU-2 allows ≤2,000 sq ft bars by right, larger needs CUP[1][8]
  • Advantage: Highest revenue per seat; natural fit for Deep Ellum / West 7th nightlife corridors
  • Limitation: TABC licensing, liquor control overlays, age verification, noise/security expectations, higher insurance[3]

Health, facility & build-out

Energy drink shop

Similar to coffee: Handwash sink, 3-compartment if reusable ware, commercial refrigeration, ice program, sanitizer station.[2]

Unique: Caffeine mg tracking/display; high-volume ice; tap/dispense for bases; functional add-in storage (collagen, vitamins).

Lighter than bar: No bar wells, no age-gated entry, no dram shop liability for alcohol service.

Coffee shop

Heaviest equipment: Espresso machine ($8K–25K+), grinders, hot water, pastry case.[2]

Food complexity: Often adds bakery/kitchen — increases health risk tier and grease interceptor need.

Simplest path: Beverage-only coffee + prepackaged food stays lower risk classification.

Alcohol bar

Additional requirements: TABC-compliant layout, age verification systems, security plan in some SUPs, often larger occupancy loads.[1]

Food minimums: Texas requires food service with alcohol in many permit types — kitchen or prepackaged food program.

Highest scrutiny: Noise, outdoor speakers, patio hours often conditioned in SUP (e.g., no outdoor speakers past midnight).[6]

Where VOLT BAR wins vs. coffee vs. bar

vs. Coffee shop

Win: Differentiated niche (custom energy, mg transparency), stronger late-night model, higher ticket potential, gaming/fitness culture — less direct competition with Starbucks/Dutch Bros on morning commute.

Trade-off: Coffee has simpler zoning acceptance, lower education burden for inspectors, and proven landlord demand. Energy shop is newer category — may need to explain use to planning staff.[4]

vs. Alcohol bar

Win: No TABC, no liquor overlay, no age gate, no 75% alcohol revenue trap, restaurant-by-right in Deep Ellum PD 269[7], lower liability, can serve under-21 gaming crowd.

Trade-off: Lower per-drink revenue than cocktails, no "bar scene" alcohol markup, must build vibe without alcohol as anchor. Bar corridors already expect nightlife infrastructure (security, SUP conditions).

Recommended classification for VOLT BAR

Operate as a restaurant without drive-in service (food/beverage establishment) — same zoning bucket as a coffee shop, not a bar. Pursue late-hours SUP only if targeting midnight–2 AM in PD 842 or proposed Deep Ellum rules.[5][6] Do not add alcohol without rezoning/TABC analysis — crossing 75% alcohol revenue reclassifies the use in Dallas and Frisco.[1][9]
